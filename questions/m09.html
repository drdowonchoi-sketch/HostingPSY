In Texas schools, Who can make a referral for evaluation?

Answer key

A referral or request for a full individual and initial evaluation (FIIE) may be made at any time by school personnel, the student's parent or legal guardian, or another person involved in the education or care of the student. If the school suspects a disability and a possible need for special education, it must refer the student for an evaluation. A student does not have to receive interventions or support services for any particular length of time before a referral is made.

Facts an answer must state: school personnel · parent · education or care  ·  Governing law: TAC §89.1011

Who can make a referral: In Texas, a referral or request for a full individual and initial evaluation (FIIE) may be initiated at any time by school personnel, the student's parents or legal guardian, or another person involved in the education or care of the student [6]. TEA guidance states the same [1]. Under IDEA, either a parent or a public agency may initiate a request for an initial evaluation [2].

Related requirements:

  • Referral by district personnel: District personnel must refer a student for an FIIE if the student continues to experience difficulty in the general education classroom despite interventions and support services, or at any time they suspect a disability and a possible need for special education and related services [6].
  • No pre-referral prerequisite: A district cannot require a student to participate in interventions for any specific length of time before a referral or FIIE [6]. An LEA may not deny a referral or delay an initial evaluation because pre-referral interventions have not been implemented [1].
  • Written parent requests: If a parent submits a written request to the district's director of special education services or to a district administrative employee, such as a campus principal, the district must act no later than the 15th school day after receiving it [6][1]. It must provide either:
  • prior written notice of its proposal to evaluate, consistent with 34 CFR §300.503, along with the procedural safeguards notice required by 34 CFR §300.504 and the TEA Overview of Special Education for Parents form [6]; or
  • prior written notice of its refusal to evaluate, consistent with 34 CFR §300.503, along with the same Overview form and procedural safeguards notice [6].
  • Language of the request: A written request can be made in a language other than English [1].
  • Verbal requests: A request may be made verbally and need not be in writing. A verbal request does not trigger the 15-school-day response timeline, but LEAs must still comply with federal prior written notice and procedural safeguard requirements. TEA encourages LEAs to respond to verbal requests within 15 school days and to ask the requester to put the request in writing [1].
  • Consent: If the LEA agrees to evaluate, it must give the parent the opportunity to provide written consent [1]. Consent requirements under 34 CFR §300.300 apply to initial evaluation requests [2].

In Texas, a referral or request for a full individual and initial evaluation (FIIE) for special education may be made by:

1. The student's parent or legal guardian

2. School personnel

3. Another person involved in the education or care of the student [1][3]

19 TAC §89.1011(a) states that a referral or request for an FIIE "may be initiated at any time" by any of these persons [3]. Under federal law, either a parent or a public agency may initiate a request for an initial evaluation [2]. This is consistent with 34 CFR §300.301(b), which is cited in the corpus [2].

Additional points relevant to who refers and how:

  • Duty of school personnel: If the student continues to experience difficulty despite interventions and support services, or at any time district personnel suspect a disability and a possible need for special education, district personnel must refer the student for an FIIE [3]. A school has an affirmative duty to seek consent and conduct an initial evaluation whenever it suspects a child has a disability and needs special education and related services under IDEA [4].
  • No pre-referral prerequisite: An LEA may not deny a referral or delay an initial evaluation because pre-referral interventions have not been implemented [1]. A district cannot require a student to participate in interventions for any specific length of time before a referral or FIIE [3][5]. RtI strategies may not be used to delay or deny a timely evaluation [4]. Parents can request a referral regardless of whether the child is receiving RtI interventions [4].
  • Written parent requests: If a parent submits a written request to the district's director of special education services or to a district administrative employee (such as a campus principal), the district must, not later than the 15th school day after receiving it, provide either:
  • prior written notice of its proposal to evaluate (consistent with 34 CFR §300.503), a copy of the procedural safeguards notice (34 CFR §300.504), and the Overview of Special Education for Parents form; or
  • prior written notice of its refusal to evaluate, along with the same procedural safeguards notice and Overview form [3].

The written request may be in a language other than English [1]. If the LEA agrees to evaluate, it must also give the parent the opportunity to give written consent [1][4].

  • Verbal requests: A request may be made verbally and need not be in writing. A verbal request does not trigger the 15-school-day response timeline, but the LEA must still comply with federal prior written notice and procedural safeguards requirements. LEAs are encouraged to respond within 15 school days and to ask the requester to put the request in writing [1][4].
  • Children who may be underserved: The TEA guidance lists groups for whom child find is particularly relevant, including students who are homeless, highly mobile (including migrant), in foster care, involved in the criminal justice system, or in residential placements, as well as students identified as gifted/talented who are suspected of having a disability [1].

In Texas, a referral for a special education evaluation can come from several people:

  • The parent or legal guardian. A parent can request an evaluation at any time, and the request should be in writing (email counts). The school must respond within 15 school days, either by giving the parent prior written notice and a consent form for evaluation, or by refusing in writing with an explanation. If the parent still disagrees, they can use procedural safeguards such as mediation or a due process complaint.
  • School personnel. Teachers, counselors, administrators, and other staff who work with the student can refer, usually after general education interventions (such as RTI/MTSS) haven't resolved concerns. Schools also have a "Child Find" duty to identify students who may have a disability.
  • Another person involved in the student's education or care. This can include a physician, a child care provider, a social worker, or an agency representative, and they typically make the referral through the school or the parent.
  • A student age 18 or older, who holds the decision-making rights themselves, unless a guardian has been appointed.

Once the parent gives written consent, the district generally has 45 school days to complete the evaluation. A referral by itself doesn't start the evaluation. The parent's consent does.

Referrals for a Section 504 evaluation (a separate process for accommodations) and for dyslexia screening or evaluation can also come from parents or school staff.

I'm not a lawyer, and these timelines have some exceptions (for example, around summer breaks or a student's absence). For a specific situation, the district's special education department or the Texas Education Agency's guidance, such as the Legal Framework or Partners Resource Network (PRN), is a good place to confirm details.

In Texas, a referral for a special education evaluation can come from several sources. Federal law (IDEA) and the Texas Administrative Code (19 TAC §89.1011) allow:

  • Parents or legal guardians. This is the most common route. A parent can request an evaluation at any time, whether or not the school has concerns.
  • School personnel, such as teachers, counselors, administrators, or other staff who suspect a student may have a disability and need special education services.
  • The school district itself, acting as the "public agency" under Child Find obligations, which require districts to identify, locate, and evaluate students who may have disabilities.
  • Other people or agencies who know the child. This can include physicians, early childhood intervention (ECI) programs, child care providers, or other community or state agencies, typically with the parent's involvement.

Timelines (for initial special education evaluations)

  • A parent's request is best made in writing to the principal, special education director, or diagnostician, since that creates a clear record and starts the clock.
  • Within 15 school days of receiving a written request, the district must either give the parent prior written notice, a copy of procedural safeguards, and an opportunity to consent to the evaluation, or give prior written notice explaining why it's refusing.
  • Once the parent gives written consent, the district generally has 45 school days to complete the evaluation.

Other points

  • Referral for special education is separate from Section 504 evaluations and from dyslexia screening and evaluation, though the same people can generally initiate those.
  • Districts can't require a student to go through interventions like RTI/MTSS before evaluating if a disability is suspected. Those can run alongside, but they can't be used to delay an evaluation.
  • If the district refuses, parents can request mediation, file a state complaint with TEA, or request a due process hearing.
