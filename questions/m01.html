In Texas schools, How should communication needs be considered for deaf children?

Answer key

When developing the IEP, the ARD committee must consider the communication needs of every child with a disability. For a child who is deaf or hard of hearing, it must consider the child's language and communication needs; opportunities for direct communication with peers and professional personnel in the child's language and communication mode; the child's academic level; and the child's full range of needs, including opportunities for direct instruction in the child's language and communication mode. This is one of the special factors the ARD committee must address.

Communication needs of a student who is deaf or hard of hearing must be considered as a special factor in IEP development.

Federal requirement (IDEA)

  • In developing the IEP, the IEP Team must consider the communication needs of the child [5].
  • For a child who is deaf or hard of hearing, the team must consider the child's language and communication needs, opportunities for direct communication with peers and professional personnel in the child's language and communication mode, academic level, and full range of needs, including opportunities for direct instruction in the child's language and communication mode [5] (34 CFR §300.324(a)(2)(iv)).
  • The team must also consider whether the child needs assistive technology devices and services [5].

Texas implementation

  • The ARD committee is the IEP team defined in federal law, including 34 CFR §300.321. The district is responsible for the functions under 34 CFR §§300.320-300.325 and TEC §29.005 (individualized education programs) [8].
  • The ARD committee must include a teacher certified in the education of students who are deaf or hard of hearing when the student is suspected or documented to be deaf or hard of hearing [12].
  • TEA technical assistance states that the ARD committee shall consider the following [1]:
  • The student's language and communication needs (primary language), both receptively and expressively. Multiple modes of communication may exist in each area. The student's unique communication mode should be respected, used, and developed to an appropriate level of proficiency [1].
  • Direct communication opportunities with peers and other DHH role models, including academic and functional opportunities at school. "Direct" means person to person, not through an additional source such as an educational interpreter or captioner [1].
  • Supports needed to achieve grade-level instruction and continue proficiency in grade-level academic skills [1].
  • The full range of needs, such as functional skills (self-advocacy), related services, counseling, Hearing Assistive Technology devices, and access to extracurricular activities [1].
  • Title 2 communication requirements may affect how some of these services are delivered. They are not necessarily an IEP requirement but an Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) requirement [1].

Legislative findings on communication modes

  • Texas law recognizes that students who are deaf or hard of hearing may choose among a variety of language modes and languages, including oral and manual-visual language, American Sign Language, English-based manual-visual languages, cued speech, and spoken and written English with amplification [2].
  • The legislature recognizes that these students should have the opportunity to develop proficiency in English, including oral or manual-visual methods of communication, and in American Sign Language [2].

Placement following the IEP

  • After the IEP is developed, the ARD committee determines service intensity and placement. A continuum of placement options must be reviewed, with general education considered first [1].
  • The committee uses all of the data, including present levels of performance (PLAAFPs), special factors, goals, supplementary aids and services, and services, to decide whether the IEP can best be implemented on the home campus, a Regional Day School Program for the Deaf, the Texas School for the Deaf, or another appropriate site [1].
  • Each year, before placement decisions, the district must give the parent of an eligible DHH student the TSD information packet in writing. Best practice is to document the discussion in the IEP/service plan (birth-2) [1].

Communication needs of a deaf or hard of hearing (DHH) student must be considered by the IEP/ARD committee as a required special factor, and the evaluation must also be conducted in the student's mode of communication.

1. IEP Team requirement (federal)

  • The IEP Team must consider the child's communication needs. For a child who is deaf or hard of hearing, it must consider [1]:
  • the child's language and communication needs;
  • opportunities for direct communication with peers and professional personnel in the child's language and communication mode;
  • the child's academic level and full range of needs;
  • opportunities for direct instruction in the child's language and communication mode.
  • The team must also consider whether the child needs assistive technology devices and services [1].

2. ARD committee considerations (TEA technical assistance)

In addition to accommodations and/or modifications, the ARD committee shall consider [2][8]:

  • Language and communication needs (the student's primary language), both receptively and expressively. There may be multiple modes of communication in each area. The guidance states that DHH students must have an education in which their unique communication mode is respected, used, and developed to an appropriate level of proficiency [2][8].
  • Direct communication opportunities with peers and DHH role models, including academic and functional opportunities at school [2].
  • "Direct" communication occurs person to person, not through an additional source such as an educational interpreter or captioner [2].
  • Title 2 communication requirements may affect how some services are delivered. They are an Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) requirement and not necessarily an IEP requirement [2].
  • Supports needed to achieve grade-level instruction and continue proficiency in grade-level academic skills [2].
  • Full range of needs, such as functional skills (self-advocacy), related services, counseling, hearing assistive technology devices, and access to extracurricular activities [2].

The TEA guidance suggests the committee ask [8]:

  • What is the student's primary language and mode of communication?
  • What communicative needs and opportunities does the student have?
  • Can the student comprehend what is said in school?
  • Does the student have the skills and strategies (e.g., self-advocacy, social skills) to meet those needs and use those opportunities?
  • Can the student communicate in different settings, including social situations and noisy learning environments?
  • Does the student communicate effectively enough to participate fully in class, and if not, why not?
  • How would the deficit in communication be described?

3. Evaluation data informing the decision

  • Evaluations must be provided and administered in the child's native language or other mode of communication, in the form most likely to yield accurate information, unless clearly not feasible [13].
  • Assessments for a child with impaired sensory, manual, or speaking skills must reflect the child's aptitude or achievement rather than the impairment [14].
  • Evaluation data for eligibility based on an auditory impairment must include an audiological evaluation by a licensed audiologist [8].
  • The statewide plan ensures each student is assessed thoroughly to ascertain their potential for communication through a variety of means, including oral or aural means, fingerspelling, or sign language [8].
  • A communication assessment is typically given by the teacher of the deaf with the speech-language pathologist to identify language and communication needs [8].
  • Data from the FIE help identify additional factors to add to the IEP [8].

4. Texas policy context

  • The legislature recognizes that DHH students may choose oral and/or manual-visual language modes, including ASL, English-based manual systems, cued speech, and speech with amplification. It also finds that these students should have the opportunity to develop proficiency in English and ASL [3].
  • Interpreting services for students who are deaf must be provided by an interpreter certified in the appropriate language mode(s), if certification is available. The interpreter must be certified by or a certified member of the Registry of Interpreters for the Deaf or the Texas Board for Evaluation of Interpreters [7].

5. Placement

  • After the IEP is developed, the ARD committee determines service intensity and placement. A continuum of placement options must be reviewed, with general education considered first [2].
  • Options include the home campus, a Regional Day School Program for the Deaf, or the Texas School for the Deaf [2]. Each year, before placement decisions, the district must give the parent of an eligible DHH student the TSD information packet in writing [2].

The context does not specify a single required communication mode or method. It emphasizes individualized consideration of the student's own language and communication mode.

Short answer: Communication is treated as a central, individually assessed factor — not an add-on accommodation — for Texas students who are deaf or hard of hearing (DHH). It is driven by the Texas "Deaf Child's Bill of Rights," the ARD (IEP) committee process, and a required communication assessment.

The governing frameworkThe Texas Deaf Child Bill of Rights, Texas Education Code §§29.300–29.315, is state law recognizing the unique communication and language needs of students who are deaf or hard of hearing. Under TEC §29.303, students who are deaf or hard of hearing must have an education in which their unique communication mode is respected, used, and developed to an appropriate level of proficiency. The stated purpose includes ensuring all DHH students have appropriate, ongoing, and fully accessible educational opportunities (TEC §29.302).

What schools must actually do

Evaluate communication, not just hearing. Eligibility data must include an audiological evaluation by a licensed audiologist plus a communication assessment completed by the multidisciplinary team, and must describe the implications of the hearing loss across a variety of circumstances, with and without recommended hearing assistive technology. The speech-language pathologist and the teacher of students who are deaf or hard of hearing should conduct the communication assessment cooperatively.

Address communication in every part of the IEP. ARD committees must carefully consider the student's unique needs, treating communication access as a major factor in all areas of the IEP and the educational experience. IDEA's "consideration of special factors" for DHH students is implemented in Texas through the Auditory Impairment (AI) / DHH Supplement to the IEP. Documented special factors include the student's communication needs, language needs for students with limited English proficiency, and behavioral supports where behavior impedes learning.

Consider the full range of modes. Texas Education Code requires addressing aural, oral, fingerspelling, and sign language options.

Provide direct communication and peer/role-model access. Any educational program for DHH students must help meet their unique communication needs and give them access to students who use the same language mode and to role models who are also DHH. Regional Day School Programs for the Deaf (RDSPD) provide centralized supports — interpreting, auditory supports, communication supports, services from a teacher of the deaf and hard of hearing, and other related services needed for a free appropriate public education.

Services start early. Under TEC §29.003, a free appropriate public education must be available from birth to students who are deaf or hard of hearing.

Communication access for parents too. Districts must take all reasonable actions to ensure the parent understands ARD proceedings, including arranging an interpreter for parents who are deaf or hard of hearing.

Answer, as graded

In Texas, communication is treated as a core, legally protected element of a deaf student's education — not just an accommodation.

1. The Texas Deaf Child's Bill of RightsThe Texas Deaf Child Bill of Rights, TEC §§29.300–29.315, is a specific state law recognizing the unique communication and language needs of students who are deaf or hard of hearing (DHH). Under TEC §29.303, students who are deaf or hard of hearing must have an education in which their unique communication mode is respected, used, and developed to an appropriate level of proficiency. TEC §29.302 directs that all students who are deaf or hard of hearing have appropriate, ongoing, and fully accessible educational opportunities. State law requires addressing aural, oral, fingerspelling, and sign language modes. (TEC Ch. 29)

2. Communication assessment at eligibilityEligibility as deaf or hard of hearing requires an otological examination and an audiological evaluation by a licensed audiologist, and the evaluation data must describe the implications of the hearing loss for the student's hearing in a variety of circumstances, with or without recommended amplification (19 TAC §89.1040(c)(3)). TEA has proposed adding a communication assessment completed by the multidisciplinary team to that requirement. TEA guidance says the speech-language pathologist and the teacher of students who are deaf or hard of hearing should conduct the communication assessment cooperatively, asking questions such as the student's primary language and mode of communication; what communicative needs and opportunities the student has; whether the student can comprehend what is said in school; and whether the student has self-advocacy and social skills to meet those needs.

3. ARD committee composition Decisions are made by the ARD (admission, review, and dismissal) committee — Texas's term for the IEP team. If a student is suspected or documented to be deaf or hard of hearing, the ARD committee must include a teacher certified in the education of students who are deaf or hard of hearing; for deaf-blindness, both a DHH-certified and a VI-certified teacher must be included. For a child from birth through age two who is deaf or hard of hearing, an individualized family services plan (IFSP) meeting is held in place of an ARD meeting.

4. Direct communication access and peer/role-model accessAny educational program for DHH students must help meet their unique communication needs and give them access to students who use the same language mode and to role models who are also deaf or hard of hearing. Regional Day School Programs for the Deaf (RDSPD) provide centralized supports: interpreting, auditory supports, therapy services, transportation, communication supports, and services from a teacher of the deaf and hard of hearing. Any student whose hearing impairment severely impairs processing linguistic information through hearing, even with recommended amplification, and adversely affects educational performance, is eligible for RDSPD consideration subject to ARD committee decisions.

5. Language-acquisition monitoring, ages 0–8TEC §29.316 requires that language acquisition for DHH or deafblind children be closely monitored from birth through age eight so timely interventions support age-appropriate language skills. LEAs administer an approved assessment of expressive and receptive language in English or ASL; some tools assess ASL and some are available in multiple languages to honor the child's preferred communication mode at home. Data is submitted to the Special Education Language Acquisition (SELA) collection in TSDS.

6. Communication access for deaf parentsThe district must take all reasonable actions to ensure the parent understands the ARD meeting, including arranging an interpreter for parents who are deaf or hard of hearing.

Practical takeaway for an ARD meeting: ask the committee to document the student's primary language and communication mode, the communication assessment results, how direct communication with peers and staff will occur in that mode, whether amplification/interpreting/CART is needed, and access to DHH peers and adult role models.

Contact: TEA Special Education, (512) 463-9414, tea.texas.gov sensory impairments. Note that state sensory plans are under review as TEA transitions to a consolidated state plan under TEC 30.002 as updated by House Bill 2 (89th Legislature), so verify current plan language with TEA.

This is informational only, not legal advice. For a dispute over an individual IEP, consult a special-education attorney or an advocacy organization.
