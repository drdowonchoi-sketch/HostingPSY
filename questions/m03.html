In Texas schools, What are schools required to do for disabled children?

Answer key

Schools must find, identify, locate, and evaluate children suspected of having a disability who may need special education (child find), including responding to a parent's request for an evaluation, whether made in writing or verbally. For a child who is eligible, the school must provide a free appropriate public education (FAPE) in the least restrictive environment through an IEP developed by the ARD committee. Throughout, the school must follow procedural safeguards, including giving prior written notice and obtaining parent consent for the initial evaluation and the initial provision of services.

Based on the excerpts available, Texas schools must provide a free appropriate public education (FAPE) to children with disabilities under both IDEA and Section 504, and must carry out specific procedural and implementation duties. The excerpts are partial, so this is not a complete list of every obligation.

1. Provide FAPE

  • IDEA: The purpose of Part 300 is to ensure that all children with disabilities have a FAPE that emphasizes special education and related services designed to meet their unique needs and prepare them for further education, employment, and independent living [15]. TEA's materials restate this standard [2][10][11].
  • Section 504: A recipient operating a public elementary or secondary program must provide a FAPE to each qualified person in its jurisdiction, regardless of the nature or severity of the disability (34 CFR §104.33(a)) [12].
  • An appropriate education means regular or special education and related aids and services designed to meet individual needs as adequately as the needs of nonhandicapped persons are met [12][3].
  • Implementing an IEP developed under the Education of the Handicapped Act is one means of meeting this standard [12].
  • Education must be provided without cost to the student or parents, except for fees imposed on nondisabled students [12].
  • A recipient may not exclude any qualified student from public elementary or secondary education [13].
  • Other Section 504 protections: Students must receive an equal opportunity to participate in athletics and extracurricular activities, and must be free from bullying and harassment based on disability [3].

2. Develop and implement an IEP through the ARD committee

  • An ARD committee completes the IEP for every public-school child ages 3–21 receiving special education services in Texas [2][10].
  • The ARD meeting must be held at a mutually agreeable time and place. The school must take steps to ensure that one or both parents are present or are given the opportunity to participate [2][10].
  • Initial IEPs: The IEP meeting must occur within 30 days of a determination that the child needs special education and related services. Services must be made available as soon as possible after the IEP is developed (34 CFR §300.323(c)) [7].
  • Content: Texas law requires the written IEP statement to include only the information in the TEA model form, and use of the model form is optional [2][14].

3. Make the IEP accessible and implemented

  • The IEP must be accessible to each regular education teacher, special education teacher, related services provider, and other responsible service provider. Each must be informed of their specific responsibilities and of the accommodations, modifications, and supports required (34 CFR §300.323(d)) [7].
  • Texas districts must also ensure that each teacher has access to relevant IEP sections and has an opportunity to request implementation assistance [9].
  • Districts must have a process for teachers in general education settings to request an IEP review and provide input. It must include a timely district response and notification of the parent or guardian [9].
  • Transfers within Texas: The new district, in consultation with the parents, must provide FAPE, including services comparable to the prior IEP, until it adopts the prior IEP or develops a new one (§300.323(e)) [7].
  • Transfers from another state: The excerpt is cut off, so this requirement is not covered here [7].

4. Provide specific services and conditions

  • Physical education: Physical education, specially designed if necessary, must be made available to every child with a disability receiving FAPE. This does not apply if the agency enrolls nondisabled children in the same grades and does not provide them physical education (§300.108) [6].
  • Children must have the opportunity to participate in regular PE unless they are enrolled full time in a separate facility or their IEP prescribes specially designed PE [6].
  • Instructional day: Students with disabilities must have an instructional day commensurate with that of nondisabled students. The ARD committee determines the setting and length of day, and the IEP must specify them [9].
  • Residential placement: If a residential placement is necessary to provide FAPE, it must be provided at no cost to the student or parents, including non-medical care and room and board (§104.33(c)(3)) [13].
  • Transportation: If the recipient places or refers a student to services it does not operate, it must ensure adequate transportation at no greater cost than if the student were in its own program (§104.33(c)(2)) [13].
  • Placements outside the district: A nonpublic day school placement through a contractual agreement is used when the district cannot provide FAPE [8]. For students at the Texas School for the Blind and Visually Impaired, the resident district remains responsible for FAPE and for conducting all legally required IEP meetings [5].

5. Maintain records, policies, and procedural safeguards

  • Each district must maintain an eligibility folder for each student receiving special education, including referral data, notices and consents, evaluation reports, ARD reports, and IEPs with supporting data [9].
  • Districts must develop policies, procedures, programs, and practices consistent with the state's policies to implement IDEA [9].
  • Schools must give parents the Notice of Procedural Safeguards at least once a year and at specified events [11]:
  • on referral or request for an initial evaluation;
  • on receipt of the first state complaint in a school year;
  • on receipt of the first due process hearing request in a school year;
  • on the day a disciplinary change of placement is decided; and
  • on parent request.
  • Schools must also give parents the TEA "Overview of Special Education for Parents" form when the child is initially referred for evaluation [11].

6. Emergency drills and safety planning

  • The commissioner must establish recommendations for accommodating students with an IEP or a Section 504 plan during mandatory school drills, and guidelines for documenting those accommodations in the district's multihazard emergency operations plan [4].
  • A district must follow those guidelines when adopting and implementing its plan [4].

Based on the retrieved excerpts, Texas schools must meet the following obligations for students with disabilities. The excerpts do not give a complete statement of every duty (see the last section).

1. Serve eligible students under IDEA

  • A "child with a disability" is a child evaluated under 34 CFR §§300.304–300.311 as having one of the listed impairments who, by reason of it, needs special education and related services [15]. The listed categories are intellectual disability, hearing impairment (including deafness), speech or language impairment, visual impairment (including blindness), emotional disturbance, orthopedic impairment, autism, traumatic brain injury, other health impairment, specific learning disability, deaf-blindness, and multiple disabilities [15]. Section 300.8(c) defines each category [16][17][18][19].
  • A child who has a listed disability but needs only a related service, not special education, is not a child with a disability under this part. The exception is where the state treats that service as special education [15].
  • Under 34 CFR §300.111(d), IDEA does not require classification by disability category, as long as each child who has a listed disability and needs special education and related services is regarded as a child with a disability [2].

2. Comply with Section 504 and Title II, even for students who are not IDEA-eligible

  • A district that finds a student ineligible under IDEA must still consider whether the student has a disability under Section 504 or Title II [2].
  • Section 504 and Title II require a physical or mental impairment that substantially limits a major life activity or bodily function. They have no disability categories and no requirement that the student need special education [2][3].
  • A child with a disability who needs only a related service may be covered by Section 504 and entitled to FAPE services under it [3].
  • All IDEA-eligible students are protected by Section 504 and Title II, but not all Section 504/Title II students are IDEA-eligible [3].
  • Title II also gives students the right to effective communication (28 C.F.R. §35.160). Auxiliary aids and services include qualified interpreters, note takers, assistive listening systems, Braille materials, captioning, and accessible technology (28 C.F.R. §35.104) [3]. Districts must comply with both IDEA and Title II [3].

3. Develop and implement an IEP through the ARD committee

  • Committee: Before a child is enrolled in special education, the district must establish a committee composed of the persons required under 20 U.S.C. §1414(d). Any required general education teacher must, to the extent practicable, be one who is responsible for implementing part of the IEP [4].
  • Timelines: A meeting to develop the IEP must occur within 30 days of a determination that the child needs special education and related services. Services must be made available as soon as possible after the IEP is developed [1].
  • Decision-making: The IEP is developed by agreement of the committee members, or by an alternate method provided by the agency if they cannot agree. Majority vote may not be used [4].
  • Documentation: The written IEP must document the committee's decisions on the issues discussed at each meeting. It must include the meeting date, the name, position, and signature of each participating member, and whether the parents, the adult student (if applicable), and the administrator agreed or disagreed [4]. TEA guidance notes that decisions documented in other IEP sections need not be restated [10].
  • Disagreement: If the IEP is not developed by agreement, it must include the basis of the disagreement. Each member who disagrees may include a statement of disagreement [4].
  • Translation: If the parent's primary language is not English, the district must provide a written or audiotaped IEP translated into Spanish if that is the parent's primary language. For any other language, it must make a good faith effort to provide a translated copy [4].
  • Required content (34 CFR §300.320):
  • Present levels of academic achievement and functional performance [12].
  • Measurable annual goals, with benchmarks or short-term objectives for students taking alternate assessments [12].
  • How progress will be measured and reported [12].
  • Special education, related services, and supplementary aids and services, based on peer-reviewed research to the extent practicable, plus program modifications or supports for school personnel [12][13].
  • An explanation of any non-participation with nondisabled peers [13].
  • Assessment accommodations, or a justification for an alternate assessment [13].
  • The projected start date, frequency, location, and duration of services [13].
  • Accessibility: The IEP must be accessible to each regular education teacher, special education teacher, related services provider, and other responsible service provider. Each must be informed of their specific responsibilities and the required accommodations, modifications, and supports [1].
  • Transfers within Texas: If a child with an IEP transfers to a new district in the same state within the school year, the new district must, in consultation with the parents, provide FAPE, including services comparable to the prior IEP. It must do so until it adopts the previous IEP or develops and implements a new one that meets §§300.320–300.324 [1].

4. Provide transition planning

  • Texas transition planning under TEC §29.011 and 19 TAC §89.1055(h) must begin no later than when the student reaches age 14. The ARD committee must consider, and if appropriate address, these issues and review them annually [10]. They include student involvement, parental involvement (or, for students 18 and older, involvement of parents and others who are invited or have consent through a supported decision-making agreement), and postsecondary issues [10].
  • Federally, beginning with the first IEP in effect when the child turns 16 (or younger if appropriate), the IEP must include measurable postsecondary goals based on age-appropriate transition assessments, and the transition services needed to reach them [14].
  • Beginning at least one year before the age of majority, the IEP must state that the child has been informed of the rights that will transfer at that age [14].

5. Ensure appropriate placement

  • The ARD committee decides whether a nonpublic day or residential placement is needed for the student to receive FAPE. In that case the district may contract with a qualifying provider [8][9].
  • Before the ARD committee places or refers a student to a nonpublic residential program, it must initiate and conduct a meeting. The excerpt cuts off before the rest of the requirement [9].
  • For students placed at the Texas School for the Blind and Visually Impaired, the district of residence remains responsible for ensuring FAPE and for conducting all legally required IEP development and review meetings [5].

6. Handle graduation and termination of services properly

  • Services must be available until the student graduates or exceeds the age eligibility under state law, which is age 21 in Texas [11]. An adult student who is 21 on September 1 is eligible through the end of that school year or until graduation with a diploma, whichever comes first [11].
  • When eligibility ends because of graduation or age, the school must give prior written notice. It must also give the student a summary of academic achievement and functional performance that includes recommendations for meeting postsecondary goals [11].

In Texas, public schools are legally required to provide children with disabilities access to an appropriate education, along with the special education services, accommodations, and protections they need to participate in school.

The exact requirements depend on the child's disability, how it affects learning or daily school activities, and whether the child qualifies for special education under federal or state law.

1. What Texas schools are required to do

Identify and evaluate children who may have a disability

Schools must take steps to identify, locate, and evaluate children who may need special education services. This is often called Child Find.

A parent can request a special education evaluation in writing.

The school cannot refuse to consider an evaluation simply because the child is passing their classes or advancing to the next grade.

The school generally must obtain informed parental consent before conducting an initial special education evaluation.

Under federal IDEA rules, the evaluation generally must be completed within 60 calendar days after receiving parental consent, subject to exceptions. Texas has additional rules for calculating school-day deadlines.

Provide special education services when a child qualifies

If a child qualifies under the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA), the school must provide a Free Appropriate Public Education (FAPE).

This can include:

Specially designed instruction tailored to the child's needs.

Speech therapy, occupational therapy, physical therapy, or other related services when required for the child to benefit from special education.

Behavioral supports and positive behavioral interventions when appropriate.

Assistive technology, communication supports, or specialized learning materials.

Transportation or other related services when necessary.

Services should be based on the child's individual needs, not simply on what services the school already has available.

Develop an Individualized Education Program (IEP)

A child who qualifies for special education must have an IEP developed by a team that includes the parent or guardian.

The IEP identifies:

The child's current academic performance and functional needs.

Measurable educational goals.

Special education services and related therapies.

Classroom accommodations and modifications.

How progress will be measured and reported.

The educational setting and any necessary supports.

The school must implement the IEP. It generally must be reviewed at least annually, and the child must be reevaluated at least every three years unless the parent and school agree a reevaluation is unnecessary.

Educate the child in the least restrictive environment

Schools must educate students with disabilities alongside students without disabilities as much as appropriate for the individual child.

This is called the Least Restrictive Environment (LRE) requirement.

For example, a school may need to provide:

Support from a special education teacher in a general education classroom.

A classroom aide or individualized assistance when necessary.

A specialized classroom for some or all of the school day if the child's needs cannot be met appropriately in a general education setting with supplementary aids and services.

A school generally cannot place a child in a separate classroom simply because the child has a disability. Placement must be based on the child's individual needs.

2. What if the child has a disability but does not qualify for special education?

The child may qualify for protection under Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act.

A Section 504 plan can provide accommodations such as:

Extra time on tests.

Preferential seating.

Frequent breaks or movement opportunities.

Accessible facilities and learning materials.

Health-related accommodations.

Adjustments to school policies when necessary because of a disability.

A child may qualify for Section 504 even if they do not qualify for an IEP. Schools must evaluate students when appropriate and provide the accommodations or services necessary for equal access to education.

3. What about behavior problems or discipline?

A school must follow applicable disability laws when disciplining a student with a disability.

Depending on the circumstances, the school may need to:

Consider whether the behavior is related to the child's disability.

Review the IEP and behavioral supports.

Conduct a functional behavioral assessment or develop a behavioral intervention plan when appropriate.

Follow special IDEA procedures when a disciplinary removal constitutes a change of placement.

4. What rights do parents have in Texas?

Parents generally have the right to:

Request an evaluation for special education or Section 504 services.

Participate in IEP and placement decisions.

Receive copies of evaluations and educational records.

Receive written notice when the school proposes or refuses certain actions.

Disagree with school decisions and pursue dispute-resolution options.

Request an IEP or ARD meeting when their child's needs are not being met.

In Texas, the special education committee is called the ARD committee (Admission, Review, and Dismissal). It develops and reviews the child's IEP.

Important Texas evaluation deadlines

15 school days: After receiving a written parental request for an initial special education evaluation, the district must respond with notice that it agrees or refuses to evaluate, along with required information about parental rights.

45 school days: Generally, the district must complete the initial evaluation after receiving written parental consent, subject to exceptions.

30 calendar days: Generally, the ARD committee must determine eligibility within 30 calendar days after the evaluation report is completed, subject to exceptions.

In Texas, public schools are required by federal and state law to provide children with disabilities access to an appropriate education. A school generally cannot refuse to educate a child, ignore a qualifying disability, or deny legally required services because providing them is inconvenient or costs more.

What the school must do depends on the child's disability, how it affects learning or school activities, and which legal protections apply.

1. What Texas schools are required to do

Identify and evaluate children who may have disabilities

Schools must take appropriate steps to identify and evaluate children who may need special education or disability-related support.

Evaluate a child when the school suspects a disability that may require special education.

Obtain informed parental consent before conducting an initial special education evaluation.

Complete a full and individual initial evaluation within applicable legal timelines.

Not delay an evaluation simply because a child is struggling academically, has behavioral problems, or is receiving intervention services.

Parents can request a special education evaluation in writing.

Provide special education and related services

Under the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA), eligible children are entitled to a Free Appropriate Public Education (FAPE).

Depending on the child's individual needs, this may include:

Specially designed instruction.

Speech, occupational, or physical therapy.

Counseling or psychological services.

Assistive technology and communication support.

Transportation or other related services when necessary.

Classroom accommodations, behavioral supports, and individualized teaching methods.

The school must develop and implement an Individualized Education Program (IEP) for an eligible child receiving special education.

Create and follow an IEP

An IEP is a legally binding educational plan developed by a team that includes the child's parent or guardian.

It generally identifies:

The child's current academic and functional needs.

Measurable educational goals.

Special education services, related services, and accommodations.

How progress will be measured and reported.

The educational setting and time spent with students without disabilities.

The school is responsible for implementing the services in the IEP. It cannot simply disregard the plan or routinely leave required services undelivered.

Provide accommodations even if the child does not qualify for special education

A child may qualify for protection under Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act or the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) even if the child does not qualify for special education under IDEA.

Depending on the child's needs, accommodations may include:

Extra time for tests or assignments.

Preferential seating or reduced-distraction testing.

Access to medication, medical care, or restroom breaks when needed.

Accessible classrooms, buildings, materials, or technology.

Adjustments to school policies when necessary to provide equal access.

A qualifying child may receive a Section 504 plan rather than an IEP. Schools must not discriminate against students because of a disability.

Educate children in the least restrictive environment

Schools must educate children with disabilities alongside students without disabilities to the maximum extent appropriate.

This means the school should not automatically place a child in a separate classroom simply because the child has a disability. A more restrictive setting may be appropriate when the child's individual needs cannot be met satisfactorily in a regular classroom with supplementary aids and services.

Protect children from disability discrimination and inappropriate discipline

Schools must follow applicable disability laws when disciplining students.

Depending on the circumstances, schools may need to consider whether behavior was related to a disability, whether the IEP was implemented, and whether special IDEA disciplinary procedures apply.

For certain disciplinary removals, IDEA requires a manifestation determination review. Schools cannot use discipline to avoid their obligations to provide special education.

A disability does not automatically excuse every behavior or prevent every suspension. The applicable protections depend on the child's eligibility, circumstances, and disciplinary history.

2. What rights do parents have in Texas?

Parents and guardians generally have the right to:

Request an evaluation for special education.

Participate in IEP/ARD committee meetings.

Receive notice of proposed or refused changes to special education services.

Review their child's educational records.

Receive information about procedural safeguards and dispute-resolution options.

Disagree with the school's decisions and pursue available dispute-resolution procedures.

In Texas, the special education team is called the Admission, Review, and Dismissal (ARD) committee. It develops, reviews, and revises the child's IEP.

For an initial special education evaluation, Texas generally requires the written evaluation report within 45 school days after the school receives the parent's written consent, subject to specific exceptions and different rules for certain school-calendar situations.
